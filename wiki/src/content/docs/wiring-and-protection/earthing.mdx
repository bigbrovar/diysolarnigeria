**What needs earthing in a solar installation, and how should it be connected?**

Earthing is the first layer of protection. Breakers, RCDs and surge protectors all rely on it. Without a working earth, a surge protector has nowhere to send the surge.

:::danger
Earthing is safety-critical and governed by electrical code. Use this page to understand what a good job looks like, and have a qualified electrician do the work and test it.
:::

## The layout

<Schematic name="earthing" />

Niyi Solar's principles:

- **Outdoor equipment goes straight to the earth rod.** Panel frames, mounting racks and aerials should each have their own run to the rod. That gives a surge the best chance of draining into the ground without spreading to other earthed equipment.
- **Indoor equipment shares one main earth busbar.** The inverter, the charge controller and their SPDs connect to the same bar. Ordinary circuits use the earth bar in the distribution board.
- **Keep connections few and direct.** Several busbars joined to each other in several places create the loops that let a surge bounce around before it drains.
- **Use crimped lugs, bolted down tight**, on the main busbar.

bigbrovar's own layout follows this: everything indoors lands on one earth busbar inside the house, and a single cable runs from it to the main earth bar outside and on to the rod.

## Cable sizes

Niyi Solar's figures:

| Connection | Size |
|---|---|
| Earth wire for a string of panels | The same size as the PV cables, and never less than 6 mm² |
| Earth busbar to the buried rod | 16 mm² |

Earth both the panels and the rack. In practice, if the panels are firmly bolted to a conductive rack, earthing the rack is usually enough. He earths at the screws that fix the panels to the rack to get both.

## Bond all the earth rods together

If a property has more than one earth rod, they must be bonded together. Niyi Solar: unbonded rods can sit at very different voltages during a surge, which puts people at risk of shock and equipment at risk of damage. Two neighbours in one building should not have separate, unbonded rods.

## How good does the rod need to be?

Many people believe a home earth rod must measure under 5 ohms. bigbrovar looked into this and found that 5 ohms and below is the requirement for industrial sites, substations and masts. For a home on a TT earthing system, the references he found put the ceiling at 200 ohms, on the condition that the installation has RCDs. The RCD is what makes a modest earth rod safe, because it trips on a small leakage current.

Getting a domestic rod much below 70 ohms can be very expensive. Have the resistance measured with a proper earth tester, and fit RCDs.

## Neutral to earth: one bond only

Niyi Solar on a point installers often get wrong:

- The neutral to earth bond exists so that breakers and RCDs trip reliably when there is a fault.
- It must exist in **only one place** in an installation. It is already made at the supply transformer, and where the code calls for another it belongs at the service entrance.
- An installer should **never make this bond inside an inverter**. If an inverter needs one, it is built in at the factory and certified.
- If you want the protection of a bond while running off-grid, it has to be made outside the inverter, with a way to make sure it is disconnected when mains returns.

Getting this wrong is behind many nuisance trips and some dangerous installations. See also [Moving house loads onto the inverter](/wiki/wiring-and-protection/moving-loads-onto-the-inverter/).
