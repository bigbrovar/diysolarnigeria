**Where do I start, and how big should each part be?**

Start with what you use, not with what a seller offers. AshipaEk0's comparison: you already know a small Tiger generator will not run a large freezer or an air conditioner, and that a Mikano is too much for a few bulbs. Solar is the same, except most people do not yet have that feel for the sizes. The audit gives you it.

<Schematic name="sizing-steps" />

The [DIY Solar Calculator](https://diysolarng.openculture.org.ng/) does this arithmetic for you. This page explains what it is doing and why.

## Step 1: audit your energy needs

List every appliance you want to run. For each, write down:

- its power in watts, from the label
- how many hours a day it runs

From that list you get two numbers: the **total watts** if everything runs at once, and the **energy per day** in watt-hours (watts × hours, added up).

AshipaEk0's advice is to overestimate a little. Samil's point for people who already own a system: without the audit, nobody can tell whether your system is too small or far too big for you.

## Step 2: the inverter

The inverter is sized from the total watts.

- **Leave headroom.** An inverter run at full capacity all the time will not last.
- **Allow for surge.** Fridges, freezers, pumps and air conditioners draw far more for a moment when they start.

Two rules of thumb are in use:

| Source | Rule | For a 3,000 W load |
|---|---|---|
| AshipaEk0 | Almost double your total load | At least 5,000 W |
| The calculator | Total load ÷ 0.8, so the inverter runs at 80% or less | 3,750 W |

The first is the safer choice when you have several motor loads. A bigger inverter does not give you more stored energy, and it uses more power just to stay on. Samil: check the standby consumption in the manual.

## Step 3: the battery

The battery is sized from the energy you use when the panels are not producing, which is mostly the night.

Samil's picture: the battery is the fuel in the tank. How fast it empties depends on the load you put on it.

How much of the battery you may use depends on the type:

| Battery | Use at most | So divide your night energy by |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium (LiFePO4) | 80% | 0.8 |
| Tubular or other lead acid | 50% | 0.5 |

Example: 2,000 Wh used overnight needs a 2,500 Wh lithium battery, or a 4,000 Wh tubular bank.

AshipaEk0 adds that for lithium this limit is really about voltage. Keep the cells between 3.0 V and 3.5 V and you are within safe limits. A quality pack should deliver its full rated energy inside that range. For tubular batteries the 50% limit is one you must respect every day. See [Getting the most from tubular batteries](/wiki/tubular-batteries/getting-the-most-from-tubular-batteries/).

## Step 4: the panels

The panels have to produce a whole day's energy in the few hours of good sun, and refill the battery while the house is also running.

- **Do not count on the sticker.** AshipaEk0 and bigbrovar both plan on panels producing about 70% of their rated power. A 600 W panel is a 420 W panel for planning.
- **Use conservative sun hours.** bigbrovar plans on 3 to 4 hours a day. The calculator uses the worst month of the year for your state.
- **Add the battery's charging loss.** About 15% for tubular. A few percent for lithium.

The sum:

**Panel watts needed = energy per day ÷ (sun hours × 0.7)**

bigbrovar's example: a home using about 2 kWh a day, refilling a tubular bank, needs about 5 kWh generated to be comfortable. A 1.5 kW array × 0.7 is about 1 kW, and 1 kW × 4 sun hours is 4 kWh on a poor day. That is slightly short, which tells you panels are the next upgrade.

Then check the panels against the charge controller and the battery: [Sizing panels to a charge controller](/wiki/panels/sizing-panels-to-a-charge-controller/).

## Check what you are buying

AshipaEk0's reminder for step 2: an inverter should carry the IEC 62109-1 and IEC 62109-2 safety certifications. Look the brand up in the [IECEE certificate database](https://certificates.iecee.org/), and photograph the rating plate before you pay. If the plate looks home-printed, walk away.
